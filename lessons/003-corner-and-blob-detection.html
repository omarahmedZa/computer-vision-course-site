Corner and Blob Detection

You can explain why features matter and detect corners and blobs using image gradients, the Harris structure tensor, non-maximum suppression, and scale-space (LoG/DoG) responses.

Why this matters

Raw pixels change drastically with viewpoint, illumination, and noise. To match, track, stitch, or register images, we need repeatable local structures — points we can find again in another image of the same scene.

Good features are distinctive and localizable. Flat regions are ambiguous, edges are ambiguous along their length, but corners and blobs often provide stable, well-localized cues. Understanding how detectors work (and when they fail) is essential before moving to descriptors like SIFT and to registration pipelines.

2×2structure tensor summarizing local gradient variation
σscale: controls what “blob size” is emphasized

The one core idea

Detect corners where intensity changes significantly in two different directions (captured by the eigenvalues/response of the local gradient structure tensor). Detect blobs as regions that stand out from surroundings at a particular scale, using scale-normalized Laplacian-of-Gaussian (LoG) or its efficient approximation Difference-of-Gaussians (DoG), followed by non-maximum suppression.

Corner: strong gradients in orthogonal directions · Blob: extremal response in space & scale

Gradients measure local change; second-moment structure measures how that change is distributed; scale-space compares responses across blur levels to find characteristic sizes.

How it actually works

1) Why features matter

Local features provide anchor points for: correspondence/matching (same point in two views), tracking (motion over time), image registration/stitching (aligning images), and as inputs to higher-level recognition. A useful feature is (ideally) repeatable under small changes, distinctive, accurately localized, and efficient to compute.

2) Image gradients and local structure

The image gradient ∇I = (I_x, I_y) (approximated by filters like Sobel/central differences) points in the direction of greatest intensity increase; its magnitude |∇I| measures edge strength.

  • Flat region: small gradients in all directions.
  • Edge: large gradient in one dominant direction, small orthogonal to it.
  • Corner/junction: large gradients in multiple directions (neighborhood shifts noticeably in more than one direction).

3) Harris corner detector

Harris measures how much a windowed patch changes when shifted by (u,v). Using a first-order approximation, the change is summarized by the second-moment (structure) matrix computed over a window W (often Gaussian-weighted):

M = \sum_{(x,y)\in W} w(x,y) \begin{bmatrix} I_x^2 & I_x I_y \\ I_x I_y & I_y^2 \end{bmatrix}

The eigenvalues λ1, λ2 of M describe gradient variation along principal directions:

  • Flat: λ1, λ2 small.
  • Edge: one large, one small.
  • Corner: both large.

Harris avoids explicit eigen-decomposition with a response function:

R = det(M) - k · (trace(M))^2 = λ1 λ2 - k(λ1+λ2)^2,   k \in [0.04, 0.06]

Pipeline: compute gradients → form M per pixel (smoothed products) → compute R → threshold → apply non-maximum suppression (NMS) so nearby strong responses yield a single well-localized corner.

4) Blob detection: LoG, DoG, and scale-space intuition

A blob is a region darker/brighter than its surroundings at some scale (size). Because the “right” size is unknown, detectors search across scale-space.

Scale-space: smooth the image with Gaussians of increasing σ: L(x,y,σ)=G(x,y,σ)*I(x,y). Larger σ removes fine detail and highlights larger structures.

Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG): the Laplacian ∇² measures second derivative (center-surround contrast). The normalized LoG, σ² ∇²L, gives strong extrema at blob centers when the filter scale matches blob size.

Difference of Gaussians (DoG): D(x,y,σ,k)=L(x,y,kσ)-L(x,y,σ) approximates the scale-normalized LoG efficiently (used in SIFT). Blobs are detected as local extrema in (x,y,σ) (space and scale), followed by refinement and thresholding/NMS-like selection.

Worked examples

Example 1 — Reading the structure tensor

Suppose in a window gradients are mostly horizontal: many I_x values large, I_y near zero. Then \sum I_x^2 is large, \sum I_y^2 and \sum I_x I_y small → M ≈ diag(large, small), one large and one small eigenvalue: an edge. If gradients vary strongly in both axes (e.g. an L junction), both diagonal terms become large → corner.

Example 2 — Harris response intuition

For a corner, λ1=5, λ2=4, k=0.05: R = 20 - 0.05*(81)=15.95 (strong positive). For an edge, λ1=5, λ2=0.2: R = 1 - 0.05*(27.04)≈ -0.352 (not a corner). The (trace)^2 term penalizes cases dominated by a single direction.

Example 3 — Why scale matters for blobs

A tiny dark dot responds strongly to a small-σ LoG/DoG but weakly once blur exceeds its size; a large dark disk responds weakly at small σ and peaks at larger σ. Searching over σ finds the characteristic scale, enabling scale-aware detection (and, in SIFT, scale-invariant description).

The trap

Common pitfalls

Edges are not corners: high gradient magnitude alone (an edge) is not enough — Harris looks for change in two directions. This is the aperture problem in feature form.

Threshold and window sensitivity: Harris depends on window/derivative smoothing and thresholds; too low → noise, too high → missed corners. NMS is needed to avoid clusters of duplicates.

Scale blindness: a single-scale detector misses blobs/corners whose natural size differs from the filter; use scale-space when size varies.

Photometric changes: raw gradient responses can vary with contrast/illumination; robust pipelines combine detection with normalization/invariant descriptors.

Retrieval check

  1. In terms of the eigenvalues of the Harris structure tensor, what distinguishes a flat region, an edge, and a corner?
    Answer

    Flat: both small. Edge: one large and one small (change mainly in one direction). Corner: both large (significant change in two directions).

  2. What is the purpose of non-maximum suppression in corner detection?
    Answer

    To keep only local peak responses so each corner is represented once with better localization, rather than many adjacent pixels exceeding the threshold.

  3. Why does blob detection search over multiple scales (σ)?
    Answer

    Because blobs can have different physical sizes; a response peaks when the filter scale matches the blob’s characteristic scale, so multi-scale search finds blobs of varying sizes.

  4. How does DoG relate to LoG in practice?
    Answer

    Difference of Gaussians (difference between Gaussian-blurred images at nearby scales) is an efficient approximation to the scale-normalized Laplacian of Gaussian used to find space-scale extrema.
