Feature Detectors/Descriptors (SIFT)

You can describe SIFT’s pipeline from scale-space extrema to descriptors and explain its invariances and basic matching.

Why this matters

Detecting an interesting point is only half the job. To match it across images, we need a description of the surrounding appearance that stays comparable when the camera moves, zooms, or lighting shifts moderately.

SIFT (Scale-Invariant Feature Transform) is a landmark pipeline that combines detection and description in a way that is robust to scale and rotation and partially robust to illumination and viewpoint changes. Its design principles — scale-space, canonical orientation, and gradient-histogram descriptors — appear throughout classical and modern vision.

The one core idea

Find repeatable keypoints as scale-space extrema (DoG), refine their position/scale and assign a canonical orientation, then encode the local neighborhood as a normalized histogram of gradient orientations. This produces descriptors that can be compared by distance, with invariance engineered into each stage.

Detect in scale-space → normalize orientation → describe gradients → match by distance

Invariance is not magic: scale comes from σ, rotation from orientation alignment, and illumination robustness from gradients and normalization.

How it actually works

1) Scale-space extrema detection

Build a Gaussian scale-space L(x,y,σ)=G(x,y,σ)*I and compute DoG images D(x,y,σ)=L(x,y,kσ)-L(x,y,σ) across octaves/scales. Candidate keypoints are pixels that are local extrema (min or max) compared to their 8 neighbors in the same scale and 9 neighbors in each adjacent scale (26 comparisons) — extrema in both space and scale.

2) Keypoint localization and filtering

Candidate locations are refined to sub-pixel/sub-scale accuracy by fitting a local model (Taylor expansion of D) and rejecting unstable points: low-contrast candidates and candidates on edges (strong in one direction, weak in other — detected via principal curvature/Hessian ratio, similar in spirit to Harris) are discarded for better stability.

3) Orientation assignment

To achieve rotation invariance, compute gradient magnitudes and orientations in a neighborhood around the keypoint at its selected scale. Build an orientation histogram (e.g. 36 bins over 360°) weighted by magnitude and a Gaussian window. The dominant peak becomes the keypoint’s canonical orientation; strong secondary peaks may create additional keypoints. Subsequent description is done relative to this orientation.

4) Descriptor construction

Take a region around the keypoint (e.g. 16×16 at the keypoint scale), rotate coordinates to the canonical orientation, and compute gradients. Divide into a grid of cells (commonly 4×4). In each cell accumulate an orientation histogram (commonly 8 bins) weighted by gradient magnitude and a Gaussian falloff from the keypoint center. Concatenate all histograms → 4×4×8 = 128 values.

Normalization: normalize the vector to unit length to reduce linear illumination changes; clamp large values (to reduce influence of non-linear illumination/specularities) and renormalize. The result is the SIFT descriptor.

5) Invariances and matching basics

Invariances (by design, approximate): scale (detect at characteristic σ, describe in scale-normalized neighborhood), rotation (canonical orientation), illumination (gradients + normalization reduce sensitivity to additive/multiplicative changes). Not fully invariant to: large affine/projective viewpoint change, severe non-rigid deformation, occlusion, or extreme lighting/nonlinear effects — but often robust enough for matching with geometric verification.

Matching: compare descriptors (e.g. Euclidean distance in 128-D). Nearest-neighbor alone is ambiguous in repetitive textures; Lowe’s ratio test keeps a match if d1/d2 < τ (e.g. 0.7–0.8) where d1,d2 are distances to best and second-best matches — a distinctive match is much closer than the runner-up. Final reliability typically comes from geometric consistency (e.g. RANSAC, Lessons 5 & 7).

Worked examples

Example 1 — Why canonical orientation helps

The same corner appears rotated by 30° in a second image. Without alignment, gradient-orientation bins would shift and descriptor distance would be large. By rotating each patch to its dominant gradient orientation before binning, both descriptors are expressed in a common local frame, making distance small despite global image rotation.

Example 2 — Ratio test intuition

Descriptor A has nearest neighbor distances d1=0.25 (correct-looking) and d2=0.26 (almost as good) in a repetitive pattern: ratio 0.96 → ambiguous, reject. Descriptor B has d1=0.22, d2=0.55: ratio 0.40 → distinctive, accept. The ratio measures confidence, not just closeness.

Example 3 — Normalization and illumination

If lighting scales intensities by 1.5 and adds a small offset, raw intensities change a lot, but gradient directions are mostly preserved and magnitudes scale proportionally; L2 normalization of the descriptor removes much of that global scaling effect, keeping descriptors comparable.

The trap

Common pitfalls

“SIFT is invariant to everything.” It is designed for scale/rotation and moderate photometric change, not large out-of-plane viewpoint, heavy blur, or occlusion. Expect failures outside its assumptions.

Descriptor distance ≠ correct match: nearest neighbors can be wrong in repetitive or textureless regions. Use the ratio test and, for geometry, RANSAC-based verification.

Keypoint count isn’t quality: many unstable/low-contrast points (if not filtered) or dense but non-distinctive points can hurt matching; stability and distinctiveness matter.

Retrieval check

  1. What problem does assigning a canonical orientation to each SIFT keypoint solve?
    Answer

    It provides rotation invariance by aligning the descriptor’s local coordinate frame to the dominant gradient direction before computing orientation histograms.

  2. Why does SIFT use histograms of gradient orientations in spatial cells rather than raw pixel patches?
    Answer

    Histograms pooled over cells capture local structure while tolerating small positional/shape changes, and gradient-based, normalized representations are more robust to illumination variations than raw intensities.

  3. What does Lowe’s ratio test check, and why is it useful?
    Answer

    It compares the best match distance to the second-best; a low ratio indicates a distinctive, unambiguous match, filtering out matches that are only marginally better than alternatives (common in repetitive patterns).

Python in Colab: implement the lesson

Run in Google Colab. This demo shows DoG scale-space extrema (simplified), orientation histograms, a toy 128-D SIFT-like descriptor, and ratio-test matching.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def gaussian_kernel(s, size=None):
    if size is None: size=int(6*s+1)|1
    r=size//2; y,x=np.mgrid[-r:r+1,-r:r+1]
    k=np.exp(-(x*x+y*y)/(2*s*s)); return k/k.sum()

def conv2(img,k):
    kh,kw=k.shape; ph,pw=kh//2,kw//2
    p=np.pad(img,((ph,ph),(pw,pw)),mode='edge'); out=np.zeros_like(img,np.float32)
    for i in range(img.shape[0]):
        for j in range(img.shape[1]): out[i,j]=np.sum(p[i:i+kh,j:j+kw]*k)
    return out

# Synthetic image
H,W=96,96
yy,xx=np.mgrid[0:H,0:W]
img=np.full((H,W),60,np.float32)
img[20:70,25:40]=200; img[30:45,35:75]=200
img[(yy-70)**2+(xx-70)**2<10*10]=220

# DoG pyramid (one octave, few scales)
sigmas=[1.0,1.6,2.56,4.096]
Gs=[conv2(img, gaussian_kernel(s)) for s in sigmas]
DoGs=[Gs[i+1]-Gs[i] for i in range(len(Gs)-1)]

# Simplified keypoint: extrema in middle DoG
D=DoGs[1]; prevD, nextD=DoGs[0], DoGs[2]
kps=[]
for i in range(1,H-1):
    for j in range(1,W-1):
        patch=np.stack([prevD[i-1:i+2,j-1:j+2], D[i-1:i+2,j-1:j+2], nextD[i-1:i+2,j-1:j+2]])
        v=D[i,j]
        if v==patch.max() and v>5: kps.append((i,j))
        if v==patch.min() and v<-5: kps.append((i,j))

# Gradients for orientation/descriptor
sobelx=np.array([[-1,0,1],[-2,0,2],[-1,0,1]],np.float32)
sobely=np.array([[-1,-2,-1],[0,0,0],[1,2,1]],np.float32)
Ix=conv2(Gs[1],sobelx); Iy=conv2(Gs[1],sobely)
mag=np.sqrt(Ix*Ix+Iy*Iy); ori=(np.arctan2(Iy,Ix)+2*np.pi)%(2*np.pi)

def descriptor(i,j):
    # toy 4x4 cells x 8 bins = 128
    desc=np.zeros(128,np.float32)
    for cy in range(4):
        for cx in range(4):
            y0=i-8+cy*4; x0=j-8+cx*4
            hist=np.zeros(8,np.float32)
            for dy in range(4):
                for dx in range(4):
                    y=y0+dy; x=x0+dx
                    if y<0 or x<0 or y>=H or x>=W: continue
                    b=int((ori[y,x]/(2*np.pi))*8)%8
                    hist[b]+=mag[y,x]
            desc[(cy*4+cx)*8:(cy*4+cx+1)*8]=hist
    n=np.linalg.norm(desc)+1e-6; desc/=n
    desc=np.clip(desc,0,0.2); desc/= (np.linalg.norm(desc)+1e-6)
    return desc

# Take a keypoint and create a "second image" rotated/shifted version of its patch conceptually
if not kps: kps=[(40,32),(70,70)]
p=kps[0]; d1=descriptor(p[0],p[1])
# simulate match candidate: similar vs different
d2=d1.copy(); d2[::3]*=0.9; d2/=np.linalg.norm(d2)+1e-6
d3=np.random.rand(128).astype(np.float32); d3/=np.linalg.norm(d3)
def l2(a,b): return np.linalg.norm(a-b)
dist12=l2(d1,d2); dist13=l2(d1,d3)
ratio = min(dist12,dist13)/max(dist12,dist13)

fig,axs=plt.subplots(1,3,figsize=(13,4))
axs[0].imshow(img,cmap='gray'); axs[0].scatter([p[1] for p in kps],[p[0] for p in kps],s=12,c='cyan'); axs[0].set_title('DoG keypoints (simplified)')
axs[1].imshow(DoGs[1],cmap='RdBu_r'); axs[1].set_title('DoG response')
axs[2].bar(np.arange(128),d1,width=1.0); axs[2].set_title('Toy 128-D descriptor')
for a in axs[:2]: a.axis('off')
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
print('dist(similar)=%.3f dist(different)=%.3f ratio=%.3f' % (dist12,dist13,ratio))
