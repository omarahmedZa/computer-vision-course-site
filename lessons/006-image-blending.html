Image Blending

You can combine overlapping images smoothly using alpha/feathering and multi-scale (pyramid/Laplacian) blending while reasoning about seams and exposure differences.

Why this matters

Stitching panoramas, compositing regions, merging exposures, or inserting objects all require placing pixels from different images into one result. Even with perfect alignment, a hard cut often reveals a seam because lighting, exposure, sensor response, or noise differ across images.

Blending controls how intensities transition across boundaries: simple alpha blends are fast and useful, while multi-scale (pyramid) blending can hide seams by blending low frequencies broadly and high frequencies narrowly — preserving detail without obvious halos.

The one core idea

Blend images by a spatially varying weight/mask. Alpha/feathering mixes pixel values directly (good when images match well). Pyramid/Laplacian blending mixes different frequency bands at different scales so large-scale color/exposure differences transition smoothly while fine details remain sharp.

I = α·A + (1-α)·B

Blending is weighted interpolation. The mask’s shape and the scale at which you blend determine whether seams, ghosting, or blur appear.

How it actually works

1) Direct alpha blending and feathering

Given images A,B and mask/alpha α(x,y)∈[0,1]:

I(x,y) = α(x,y)\,A(x,y) + (1-α(x,y))\,B(x,y)
  • Hard mask: α is 0/1 with an abrupt boundary → can leave a visible seam if exposures/colors differ.
  • Feathering: smooth/blur the mask so α transitions gradually across the boundary → reduces seams but can create a soft “ghost”/double-exposure look if content is misaligned.

Feather width matters: too narrow = seam remains; too wide = blends unrelated structures.

2) Exposure differences and photometric seams

Even geometrically aligned images may differ in brightness, white balance, vignetting, or gain. Direct averaging in the overlap can show step changes at the seam. Simple fixes include matching global means/gains before blending; more advanced pipelines adjust photometry per band — motivating multi-scale approaches.

3) Gaussian and Laplacian pyramids (intuition)

A Gaussian pyramid is a stack of progressively blurred and downsampled images G0, G1, ... capturing structure at coarser scales. A Laplacian pyramid stores band-pass details: L_i = G_i - upsample(G_{i+1}) (with L_n = G_n at the top). Summing expanded Laplacian levels reconstructs the original — i.e. image = low-frequency base + detail bands.

4) Multi-band / Laplacian pyramid blending

To blend A,B with mask M:

  1. Build Laplacian pyramids LA, LB for the images and a Gaussian pyramid GM for the mask.
  2. Blend each level: L_i = GM_i · LA_i + (1-GM_i) · LB_i.
  3. Collapse/reconstruct the blended pyramid.

Why it works: at coarse levels the mask is effectively very smooth, so low-frequency exposure/color differences transition over a wide region; at fine levels the transition is narrow, preserving sharp edges/textures without a wide ghosting zone. This often removes seams far better than single-scale feathering.

5) Seams and alignment dependence

Blending assumes corresponding content is in the same place. If images are misaligned (parallax, motion, registration error), blending smears/ghosts structures. Choosing a good seam/compositing boundary or first registering images (Lesson 7) is often necessary; blending complements, not replaces, alignment.

Worked examples

Example 1 — Alpha blend at a pixel

At one pixel, A=200, B=100. Hard edge: α=1 → 200, neighbor α=0 → 100 (abrupt jump). Feathered: α=0.75 → 175, α=0.5 → 150, α=0.25 → 125 — a smooth ramp that hides a step if structures align.

Example 2 — Why multi-scale helps exposure seams

Left image is consistently 30 intensity units brighter. A narrow feather still shows a quick brightness change; a wide feather washes out detail. Multi-scale blending transitions the “+30 brightness” component over a large area at coarse levels while keeping fine edges (which exist in both images) blended tightly, so the seam is less perceptible without excessive blur.

Example 3 — Misalignment causes ghosting

A thin edge appears at column 50 in A and column 53 in B. A 7-pixel feather averages them across the boundary, producing a faint double edge (ghost). No amount of photometric blending fixes geometric misalignment — the remedy is better registration or a seam that avoids the edge.

The trap

Common pitfalls

Blending ≠ alignment: feathering/pyramids reduce seam visibility but cannot correct parallax or registration error; misaligned content ghosts.

Mask too hard or too soft: hard masks show exposure seams; overly soft masks create halos/blur or double images in detailed regions.

Ignoring photometric differences: large exposure/white-balance mismatches may require pre-normalization in addition to blending.

Assuming pyramid blending is free: it costs more compute/memory and must be implemented carefully at boundaries and scales, but it’s often the right tool for seamless composites.

Retrieval check

  1. What is the effect of feathering the alpha mask compared to a hard (binary) mask?
    Answer

    Feathering creates a gradual transition that can hide abrupt seams from exposure/color differences, but if content is misaligned it can produce ghosting over the wider blend region.

  2. In Laplacian pyramid blending, why do low-frequency components blend over a wider region than high-frequency details?
    Answer

    The mask is represented in a Gaussian pyramid, so at coarse (low-frequency) levels the transition is effectively broader, while at fine (high-frequency) levels it remains narrower, preserving detail.

  3. Can pyramid blending fix ghosting caused by parallax/misalignment? Why or why not?
    Answer

    No. Blending combines values at corresponding pixel locations; if the same scene structure is in different positions, mixing them creates double/blurred structures. Geometric alignment (registration) or seam placement is needed.

Python in Colab: implement the lesson

Run in Google Colab. Demonstrates alpha/feather blending and a simple Laplacian-pyramid (multi-band) blend on synthetic images with an exposure difference.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def make_img(offset=0, bright=0):
    H,W=128,256
    y,x=np.mgrid[0:H,0:W]
    base = (((x+offset)//16 + y//16) % 2)*120 + 60
    detail = 20*np.sin((x+offset)*0.3)*np.sin(y*0.3)
    return np.clip(base + detail + bright, 0, 255).astype(np.float32)

A = make_img(0, 0)
B = make_img(0, 35)  # exposure difference

def feather_blend(A,B,center,width):
    H,W=A.shape; alpha=np.ones((H,W),np.float32)
    ramp=np.clip((np.arange(W)- (center-width))/(2*width+1e-6),0,1)
    alpha *= (1-ramp)
    return alpha*A + (1-alpha)*B, alpha

# Simple Gaussian/Laplacian pyramid (down/up by factor 2)
def down(img):
    k=np.array([1,4,6,4,1],np.float32)/16
    g=np.apply_along_axis(lambda m: np.convolve(m,k,mode='same'), axis=1, arr=img)
    g=np.apply_along_axis(lambda m: np.convolve(m,k,mode='same'), axis=0, arr=g)
    return g[::2,::2]

def up(img, shape):
    out=np.zeros(shape,np.float32)
    out[::2,::2]=img
    k=np.array([1,4,6,4,1],np.float32)/8
    out=np.apply_along_axis(lambda m: np.convolve(m,k,mode='same'), axis=1, arr=out)
    out=np.apply_along_axis(lambda m: np.convolve(m,k,mode='same'), axis=0, arr=out)
    return out

def gaussian_pyr(img, levels):
    G=[img]
    for _ in range(levels): G.append(down(G[-1]))
    return G

def laplacian_pyr(img, levels):
    G=gaussian_pyr(img,levels); L=[]
    for i in range(levels):
        L.append(G[i]-up(G[i+1], G[i].shape))
    L.append(G[-1]); return L

def blend_pyr(LA,LB,GM):
    return [gm*la + (1-gm)*lb for la,lb,gm in zip(LA,LB,GM)]

def collapse(L):
    img=L[-1]
    for i in range(len(L)-2,-1,-1): img = up(img, L[i].shape) + L[i]
    return img

levels=4
center, width = W//2, 20
_, alpha = feather_blend(A,B,center,width)
hard, _ = feather_blend(A,B,center,0)
feath, _ = feather_blend(A,B,center,width)
LA,LB = laplacian_pyr(A,levels), laplacian_pyr(B,levels)
GM = gaussian_pyr(alpha,levels)
pyr = np.clip(collapse(blend_pyr(LA,LB,GM)),0,255)

fig,axs=plt.subplots(2,3,figsize=(13,6))
axs[0,0].imshow(A,cmap='gray',vmin=0,vmax=255); axs[0,0].set_title('A')
axs[0,1].imshow(B,cmap='gray',vmin=0,vmax=255); axs[0,1].set_title('B (brighter)')
axs[0,2].imshow(alpha,cmap='gray'); axs[0,2].set_title('Mask (feathered)')
axs[1,0].imshow(hard,cmap='gray',vmin=0,vmax=255); axs[1,0].set_title('Hard seam')
axs[1,1].imshow(feath,cmap='gray',vmin=0,vmax=255); axs[1,1].set_title('Feather blend')
axs[1,2].imshow(pyr,cmap='gray',vmin=0,vmax=255); axs[1,2].set_title('Multi-band (pyramid)')
for a in axs.ravel(): a.axis('off')
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
